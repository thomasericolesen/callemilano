# CalleMilano knowledge model

## Purpose

CalleMilano should help guests make a decision while showing how each important claim is known. A venue’s published policy, an editor’s assessment, and a visitor’s first-hand report are different kinds of knowledge. They can inform one another, but they must not be blended into a single unqualified statement.

This three-layer model establishes distinct ownership and trust rules for:

1. **Verified facts** — what a responsible source states about the destination.
2. **Editorial recommendations** — what CalleMilano recommends, and why.
3. **Guest feedback and observations** — what an individual visitor experienced at a particular time.

The human editor-of-record retains final responsibility for published destination content, corrections, disputes, and whether uncertainty is significant enough to change a recommendation.

## The three layers at a glance

| Layer | Main question | Primary owner | Trust treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verified facts | What does an appropriate, current source establish? | Research/fact-check editor; human editor accountable | Strong for the claim and period the source actually covers; never broader than its scope. |
| Editorial recommendations | What should a guest consider, given those facts and their needs? | Editorial author and independent reviewer; human editor accountable | Reasoned guidance, not an objective fact or guarantee. Its basis should be visible. |
| Guest feedback and observations | What did a visitor personally encounter, and when? | The reporting guest for the observation; editorial team for moderation and display | Useful as a dated signal; not proof of a general condition, policy, or safety status. |

Trust belongs to a **specific claim**, not to a source or person in every circumstance. An official opening page may be strong evidence of a published schedule, but it cannot prove the door was open at a particular moment. A guest may be reliable about what they saw on one visit, but that report does not establish the permanent opening policy.

## Layer 1: Verified facts

### Purpose

Provide the dependable factual basis guests need to plan: identity, address, published hours, prices, booking terms, formal rules, facilities, access information, beach warnings, route details, and responsible contact links.

### Ownership

- The fact-checker gathers claim-specific evidence and records the source, date checked, and the exact claim it supports.
- The editor responsible for the page maintains the facts and flags volatile or disputed information.
- The human editor-of-record is accountable for deciding whether evidence is adequate, which source applies, and whether a conflict blocks publication.
- Venue, government, tourism, park, transport, and land-management bodies own their own statements. CalleMilano reports those statements; it does not speak for them.

### Update process

1. Check the source best suited to the claim. Prefer the responsible venue or authority for its own policies, current government or authority sources for regulated/live conditions, and official route/transport sources for access and schedules.
2. Record what the source says, the date checked, its applicable time period, and any limits. A seasonal timetable is not an all-year promise; an access statement for one entrance does not cover every route.
3. Recheck volatile facts at the project’s approved review interval, before the relevant season, and when a credible correction or closure report arrives.
4. If a source disappears, becomes stale, or conflicts with another suitable source, mark the fact as uncertain and start a correction check. Do not silently retain an expired claim as current.

### Trust level

High only for the precise claim the source supports. Authority and recency matter, but neither makes a source universal. For example, an official menu is strong evidence of listed prices when checked; it does not guarantee that every dish is available on a guest’s visit.

### Publication rules

- State the fact plainly and link to the source close to the claim.
- Include a check date for volatile information and describe seasonal or date-specific limits.
- Distinguish “the venue says it is accessible” from a verified description of the actual route, toilets, gradients, or equipment.
- Do not convert missing information into “no,” infer amenities from photographs, or treat a map pin as evidence of safety, service, price, or suitability.
- Use **Verification required** for a genuinely unresolved, material question. Explain what is unknown and give the guest a responsible next step where possible.
- Do not mark a place approved or published when an unresolved fact could make the outing unsuitable, unsafe, unavailable, or materially different from the page’s promise.

### Conflict handling

Keep conflicting source statements distinct until the editor resolves their scope and recency. Prefer the source with direct responsibility for the claim, but do not discard a recent, credible contradiction merely because another source is official. If the conflict remains material, show the uncertainty and hold or qualify the recommendation according to the publication gate.

## Layer 2: Editorial recommendations

### Purpose

Turn evidence into useful guidance for a real guest: who may enjoy an outing, who may prefer another option, how much time to allow, what trade-offs matter, and what practical preparation helps. This layer answers “Does this suit our party?” rather than restating a source page.

### Ownership

- The editorial author proposes recommendations using the verified facts and the defined guest audience.
- A reviewer who did not draft the page challenges fit, caveats, assumptions, and unsupported generalisations.
- The human editor-of-record approves consequential judgements, including family suitability, accessibility interpretation, safety-sensitive wording, and whether to recommend a destination despite uncertainty.

### Update process

Revisit recommendations whenever their factual basis changes, a new relevant limitation is established, or a pattern of guest observations merits a fresh fact check. Record the reasoning and keep the recommendation’s scope clear. A changed price, closure, route, or access condition can change the advice even if the prose itself remains accurate.

### Trust level

Reasoned but interpretive. It is more useful than a bare fact list, but it is not a guarantee that every child, older adult, wheelchair user, or family will have the same experience. Trust comes from a clear rationale, relevant evidence, proportionate language, and visible limits.

### Publication rules

- Label the recommendation or write it in unmistakable advice language: “We recommend,” “may suit,” or “consider another option if…”.
- Connect advice to concrete features such as visit length, menu choices, sand, steps, heat exposure, supervision rules, or booking demands.
- Avoid guarantees, demographic stereotypes, and unexplained ratings. Age guidance is not a safety certification.
- Distinguish observed or official facts from the editor’s inference. For example: “The beach has fine sand [fact]. A stroller may be difficult to push once you reach the sand [practical recommendation].”
- State who may want another option. Balanced advice builds more trust than universal claims of family-friendliness.
- Do not use guest feedback as if it were verified evidence or as a substitute for the editor’s own reasoned recommendation.

### Conflict handling

If new facts undermine the rationale, revise or withdraw the recommendation promptly. If the disagreement is about taste or preference, keep the recommendation conditional and explain the trade-off. Do not average incompatible preferences into a false consensus.

## Layer 3: Guest feedback and observations

### Purpose

Capture useful, situated knowledge that formal sources often miss: a gate that was locked during posted hours, a temporary parking restriction, a broken lift, a crowded access point, a changed menu item, or the practical experience of a route on a particular day. Community input should help CalleMilano discover what needs checking and help other guests ask better questions.

### Ownership

- The guest owns their first-hand account and is responsible for describing it honestly and within its limits.
- The editorial team owns moderation, privacy, context, and decisions about how prominently an observation appears.
- A guest report does not transfer responsibility for factual accuracy, safety advice, or editorial approval to the contributor.

### Update process

1. Invite specific, useful observations: what was encountered, where, and when; distinguish direct experience from hearsay.
2. Review submissions for relevance, clear scope, abusive content, personal information, commercial promotion, and unsupported high-risk claims.
3. Preserve the observation’s date and context. Ask for clarification when “not accessible,” “closed,” or “great for kids” is too broad to act on.
4. Route material reports to fact-checking. Check the venue or authority before changing a verified fact or recommendation.
5. Mark older operational observations as historical or remove them from prominent “recent” summaries according to the established freshness policy. Retain useful history only when it is clearly dated and still relevant.
6. Acknowledge corrections where practical and tell contributors when their report led to a fact check or page change. Do not promise that every suggestion will be published.

### Trust level

First-hand reports can be strong evidence of what one person experienced at one time and place. They are weaker evidence for a general rule or continuing condition. Hearsay, anonymous assertions without useful context, and repeated copied claims carry little evidential weight. A single credible report can be an important warning signal even when it is not enough to settle the underlying fact.

### Publication rules

- Present feedback as a visitor report, not as a CalleMilano fact: “A visitor reported on [date] that…”
- Show enough context to interpret it, such as season, access point, or type of visit, while protecting personal information.
- Do not let ratings, likes, or report volume turn opinion into fact. Do not imply that the loudest or most recent contributor represents all guests.
- Do not publish identifying personal details, unsubstantiated allegations, discriminatory content, unsafe instructions, or promotional material disguised as feedback.
- Treat safety, water quality, closures, crime, and accessibility reports as prompts for prompt verification. Do not present an unverified report as a confirmed hazard or reassurance.
- Do not show outdated observations as current. Where useful, label them “reported on [date]” and state that conditions may have changed.

### Conflict handling

Keep guest observations visible as dated, attributed observations when they remain useful, but do not rewrite the verified-fact layer from feedback alone. Escalate credible conflicts for a source check. If the issue could cause harm or a wasted trip, add a prominent uncertainty notice while checking; temporarily remove or qualify the recommendation when that is the responsible course.

## Applying the model to destination questions

| Topic | Verified facts (Layer 1) | Editorial recommendation (Layer 2) | Guest observations (Layer 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Restaurant: opening hours** | Operator’s current published hours, kitchen hours, and holiday exceptions, with check date. | Suggest booking or calling when meal times, party size, or seasonal demand make that prudent. | “We arrived at 13:30 on [date] and the restaurant was closed.” This triggers a same-day-hours check; it does not by itself rewrite the posted schedule. |
| **Restaurant: child friendliness** | Children's menu, high chairs, formal age rules, facilities, and policies only where confirmed. | Explain which menu/meal features may suit children and the trade-offs, such as long service or limited space. | A parent may report a high chair was available or a child’s meal took a long time, tied to the visit date. One account is not a guarantee. |
| **Restaurant: wheelchair access** | Confirmed entrance, route, seating, toilet, and step-free details from the venue or a careful direct check. | Explain whether the verified arrangement seems workable for a specified mobility need; avoid a blanket “accessible” label when details are missing. | A wheelchair user may describe the exact entrance or obstacle encountered. Route-specific observations are valuable but need careful, non-generalising wording and follow-up. |
| **Restaurant: food quality** | Menu, listed ingredients, stated sourcing or preparation claims, and allergens where the restaurant documents them. | Describe menu breadth and likely fit without claiming a dish is “the best” or universally liked. | Taste and service are personal opinions. Show as dated reviews or a fair summary of varied reports, never as verified quality facts. |
| **Beach: parking** | Official or operator information about lots, location, charges, restrictions, and access, checked for the relevant period. | Explain likely arrival trade-offs and suggest arriving earlier if capacity is limited, when evidence supports that advice. | A dated report of a full lot or changed fee signals a check; it should not be generalized to every day or season. |
| **Beach: nudist areas** | Official designation, local rules, or authoritative beach/municipal guidance, scoped to the exact section. | Explain which section a family may prefer and how to avoid confusion, without stigmatizing visitors. | A guest may report customary use in a particular area on a particular date. This is not proof of a legal designation or a permanent boundary. |
| **Beach: water quality** | Current competent-authority measurements, warnings, and bathing prohibitions for the relevant location/date. | Recommend following the current authority status; do not promise safe water based on a past award or clear-looking sea. | A visitor’s observation of litter, smell, or unusual water is a prompt to check/report, not a water-quality finding. |
| **Beach: family suitability** | Sand, access, slope, facilities, lifeguard calendar, rules, and current warnings. | Match those conditions to party needs, age, mobility, shade, and intended activities. | Families can report crowding, shade, or how their own children managed the visit. Keep the date and avoid universal claims. |
| **Nature route: difficulty** | Official route distance, elevation, surface, classification, access rules, and trailhead where available. | Explain effort in practical terms and who may wish to choose a shorter or easier option; identify inference as advice. | A hiker can report a washed-out section, heat, or actual time taken on a date. Check material route changes with the land manager before revising route facts. |
| **Nature route: safety** | Official closures, restrictions, emergency guidance, and authoritative hazard notices. | Give conservative preparation advice grounded in route length, exposure, conditions, and official guidance; never guarantee safety. | A report of a fall, obstruction, or hazard is a rapid alert and fact-check trigger. Keep it clearly attributed until confirmed; escalate immediately if there is a credible ongoing danger. |
| **Nature route: seasonal issues** | Official seasonal closures, fire restrictions, weather alerts, water availability, or wildlife rules. | Explain how season changes the effort or suitability and suggest alternatives where justified. | A visitor may report heat, mud, insects, or a dry fountain on a particular visit. Date and conditions matter; it does not establish the pattern for the season. |

## Resolving contradictory information

Use the same sequence for every conflict:

1. **Define the exact claim.** “Open” may mean a published schedule, an open gate, a staffed service, or access to one area. “Accessible” may mean entrance, one route, or the entire visit. Narrow it before comparing evidence.
2. **Check place, point, and time.** Make sure the sources refer to the same venue, beach section, entrance, route segment, season, and date. Many apparent conflicts are differences of scope.
3. **Assess each source for that claim.** Consider direct responsibility, first-hand status, recency, specificity, and whether the source covers policy or actual conditions. Do not rank every source with one universal hierarchy.
4. **Seek the responsible current source.** Contact the venue or authority where public material does not settle a material question. Record both the attempt and the result.
5. **Preserve unresolved evidence separately.** Do not erase the official statement or the guest report. State what each says, when it applies, and what remains unknown.
6. **Choose a proportionate guest-facing action.** Link the live official source, give a call/contact next step, add a dated observation notice, or pause/qualify the recommendation if a guest could be harmed or make a wasted trip.
7. **Recheck and close the loop.** Update the affected facts and advice when the conflict is resolved; mark obsolete notices as resolved or historical and, where appropriate, thank the contributor.

### Example: official source says open; recent guests report closed

Keep the official schedule as the venue’s **published schedule**, with its source and check date. Do not claim the venue is definitely operating. Show a notice such as:

> **Hours uncertain:** The official schedule lists the venue as open, but visitors reported finding it closed on [dates]. Call the venue before travelling; the closure has not been confirmed.

If several independent, recent reports point to an ongoing closure, make the warning prominent and pause the recommendation until the venue or responsible authority confirms current operation. Do not delete the reports simply because they conflict with the official schedule.

### Example: official source says closed; a guest says it has reopened

Keep the published closure information and date visible, label the guest account as unconfirmed, and contact the venue or authority. Do not tell guests it has reopened based on a single observation. Resume a positive recommendation only after an appropriate current source confirms the status.

### Safety and water-quality conflicts

For water quality, closures, fire risk, dangerous route conditions, or other immediate hazards, official live authority guidance governs guest instructions. A credible guest report can trigger a warning and urgent check, but it must not be presented as an official safety determination. If reliable current authority information is unavailable and the potential consequence is material, advise guests not to rely on the destination for that activity until it is checked.

## Shared wording and presentation rules

- **Verified:** A suitable source supports this exact claim; show source and check date where the fact can change.
- **Editorial recommendation:** CalleMilano’s reasoned advice based on identified facts; make it clear this is guidance, not a guarantee.
- **Guest report:** A dated first-hand account; attribute it and keep its scope narrow.
- **Verification required:** A material question remains unsettled. Say what is missing and how a guest or editor can check it.
- **Not known** is not the same as **none**; **not reported** is not the same as **does not happen**.
- Confidence should never be implied by confident writing alone. Precision, evidence, and freshness set the limit of what CalleMilano can say.

## Long-term community trust

Community growth depends on useful corrections being welcomed and handled fairly. Make it easy to report a concrete change; do not reward volume, outrage, or positive reviews over careful evidence. Give contributors clear expectations about moderation and attribution, protect their privacy, and avoid making individual guests responsible for settling disputes. Use recurring observations to improve what the guide checks, while keeping fact verification and final editorial accountability with CalleMilano.
